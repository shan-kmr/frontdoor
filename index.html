Nobody quite knows where your front door is

Least of all the software most certain about it

Six mornings a week, a letter carrier walks up 175th Street in Queens and puts mail through door number 89-19. The address is printed on utility bills, food deliveries, and possibly on a decade of birthday cards. The United States Postal Service delivers to it without incident, addressed to Jamaica, NY 11432. It is by most human measures a working address, until a computer gets involved.

The next frontier of intelligence is touted to be software orchestrating actions in the real world: sending a courier, a taxi, or an ambulance. Many such errands begin with the conversion of an address to a coordinate, which rests on shakier ground than it seems.

For most of recorded history, nobody needed a pin to find a door. The Tabula Peutingeriana, an illustrated road map of the Roman Empire, is a ribbon nearly seven metres long and a third of a metre tall, showing the official road network with its cities, seas, and distances stretching from Western Europe to India.1 As geography it seems absurd: seas squeezed to slivers, Italy lying on its side. It records the empire as sequences, the distance to each incremental stop written beside it. Traveling meant following an itinerary by each stage, asking as you went (not so different from today). Ptolemy had already noted that places could be fixed by a pair of numbers on a grid, and his Geography listed eight thousand such pairs.2 Still, for the next fifteen hundred years almost nothing of note was delivered by coordinate.

The median address is written the way the old itineraries were (street → number → flat), each step narrowing the search until it ends at a door. A pin is the other tradition: a pair of numbers on Ptolemy's grid. The converter between the two is increasingly becoming the substrate + point of failure for planet-scale movement.

Ask software that underpins much of the world's open mapping to find the same door and the answer depends, alarmingly, on the error-prone ways humans input addresses and on the private habits of the answering engine. The resulting pin is an editorial decision made deep inside software that almost nobody reads.

New York is the rare city that publishes its answer key, so for once, these private habits can be graded. The Department of City Planning maintains 967,871 address points, each placed “approximately five feet within the building footprint along street front” and about a tenth are verified by a person walking there.3

The grading gets interesting in Queens, because its addresses carry a structural quirk geocoding engines aren't often prepared for. In 1911 an engineer named Charles U. Powell imposed one numbering plan on the borough's dozens of formerly independent villages: the digits left of the hyphen name the cross street, the digits right of it the house.4 Some 94.3% of Queens's 360,356 addresses carry that hyphen, and the city's own geocoding manual is blunt about the consequence - 13-103 and 131-03 41st Avenue are different buildings with identical digits.5

Take for instance, 89-19 175th Street, Queens. Typed one way, the address returns the correct building. Typed the way many databases store it — hyphen removed and digits fused — they answer confidently with a road, and not even the same stretch of it. Two geocoders reading the identical database resolve segments of 175th Street kilometres apart.6 Swapped with the city name on the envelope (Queens → Jamaica), one goes silent and another returns the doctor's office two doors down.

Nominatim (the geocoder behind OpenStreetMap's search box)7 normalises text before matching it and its transliteration rules declare that a hyphen is a space.8 Deeper down sits the safety net some open geocoders keep for America: TIGER, the Census Bureau's file of address ranges strung along road centrelines.9 Nominatim's importer that prepares TIGER for use asks whether each house number is an integer.10 “89-19” is not an integer. The check throws it away, and benchmarked against the raw 2025 Census files, tosses 92.8% of Queens's address ranges against 0.2% in Brooklyn. Beneath even that, the database table those ranges land in declares its house-number columns as INTEGER.11 The hole is less an error in the script than the inability of the type system to hold a Queens address.

When an address is written the way the database expects, the engines agree; when it is not, they diverge. One goes silent, one names the wrong door confidently, and only Pelias, falling back to a road, stamps the answer a fallback and docks its own confidence down to 0.8.12 It performs best partly because one of its sources is the city's live file,13 while the other two share a single frozen copy of the same file.14 The rest is editorial policy.

The borough's postal identity fares no better. The city on the envelope (Jamaica) does not exist in OpenStreetMap's ontology.15 A sweep of the entire city's data finds no place object by that name apart from the AirTrain station and the LIRR hub. A geocoder would struggle to match a word whose only referents are fragments (a building tag, a railway station) so every engine reading this map copes in its own editorial style.

Nearly nine centuries ago, Roger II of Sicily built his great map by a method of consensus: al-Idrisi questioned travellers singly and then together, and only what their separate accounts agreed on was engraved onto a great disk of silver.16 Modern-day software may have to relearn similar methods.

NYC holds a second quirk. New York sells vanity addresses — an address that deviates from the street-numbering system, for a price. If you're a developer reading this, you can petition the borough president (starting at $2,500 in the Bronx) for the right to wear an address from a street grander than the one your building fronts.17 The city keeps track of which addresses are matters of prestige in a column called b7sc_vanity.

The same experiment at full scale = 967,871 addresses × two spellings. A door counts as found when the pin lands within 100 metres of it (another variable with material influence on the results).

Accuracy can have varying interpretations. The city's five-feet-inside-the-facade point is one definition of where an address is. The building's centroid is another; the parcel, a third; the door, a fourth; the spot where delivery drivers actually stop (the definition Amazon learns from millions of packages), a fifth.18 Even New York's own methods disagree with one another by some 29 metres. Truth, in addressing, is a distribution. The referee simply has the narrowest error bars and the honesty to publish them.

Unfortunately, failure looks identical to success. Fuse the digits in a house number and the engine hands you a road two kilometres away with the same certainty it brings to a rooftop. Only Pelias stamps its guess as a guess. The non-deterministic systems soon to operate geocoders may find this detail relevant.

Nonetheless, everything above was possible because New York maintained and published an incredible answer key. Most of the world has nothing to check a pin against, and there is more than one way to have nothing.

A Cartesian product is forming of who commands the action and who takes it {person, agent, robot}. Today a human somewhere in the chain calls ahead, looks around, catches a wrong pin or recalls from memory. An agent dispatching a robot (or worse, an ambulance) with a confidently wrong coordinate urgently needs a verification layer where humans currently stand.

New York shows the layer is buildable: where address-to-coordinate mapping exists in machine-readable form, checking a pin is possible. The last-mile truth was never written down. That is the harder problem, and it is the one worth building for. ■

Postscripts

  1. re: the Queens hyphen. Nominatim’s hyphen-to-space rule is lossless: it flattens the city’s file and the incoming query alike, so a typed 89-19 and the stored 89 19 still meet, and the intact spelling returns the true building. The lossy act is fusing the digits into 8919 which may erroneously happen upstream, in software and schemas that cannot hold a hyphen before the engine ever sees the address. Once fused, no housenumber source matches, the census-range net is torn in Queens (its ranges are hyphenated too).
  2. How answers were scored. Correct = the engine returned the queried house number with a pin within 100 m of the city’s coordinate; Silent = no answer at all. Labelled = wrong but stamped: Pelias marks these match_type: fallback/interpolated and lowers its confidence. Confident-wrong = a wrong door asserted plainly; Nominatim and Photon expose no flag that could say otherwise.
  3. On the choice of Queens. A fair objection may be that if you type an address wrong, of course the pin is wrong. But none of the spellings tested here are typos (some could be, imagine what happens then). The intact form works everywhere. 8919 is how an integer-typed column stores 89-19. Even correct-by-convention input yields wrong output delivered with full confidence.
  4. On scale. The Queens hyphen and vanity addresses are just two quirks in one city. There are more in the same city: Manhattan avenue numbers are uncoupled from the grid that finding the cross street takes a per-avenue formula once printed in the front of the phone book. Brooklyn, stitched from once-independent towns, runs the same ordinal as five disconnected streets of 12th, East 12th, West 12th, Brighton 12th, North 12th. Marble Hill is legally Manhattan, physically attached to the Bronx, and carries a Bronx ZIP. Multiply these quirks at country-scale and then by the world.
